## Cell 6 -- Section 2: Byte-Level BPE Tokenizer (Markdown)

> Here is a fundamental question: how do you turn text into numbers? Computers operate on numbers, not characters. We need a systematic, invertible mapping from text to integer sequences.
> 
> The answer is a tokenizer, and we use Byte Pair Encoding -- BPE. This is the same algorithm family used by GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, LLaMA, and Claude. The idea was adapted from data compression (Gage, 1994) and applied to NLP subword segmentation by Sennrich et al. (2016).
> 
> The algorithm is elegantly simple. Start with individual bytes -- every character in every language can be represented as one or more bytes in UTF-8. That gives us a base vocabulary of 256. Then iterate: count all adjacent pairs in the corpus, find the most frequent pair, merge them into a new token, repeat. After 3836 merges we reach our target of 4096 tokens.
> 
> Why byte-level? Because it can handle any text. Chinese characters, emoji, code, binary data -- there is never an "unknown token" problem. The byte fallback guarantees lossless round-trip encoding for any valid UTF-8 input.
> 
> Our vocabulary layout is: tokens 0 through 3 are special tokens -- PAD, BOS (beginning of sequence), EOS (end of sequence), UNK (unknown). Tokens 4 through 259 are raw bytes. Tokens 260 and above are learned BPE merges.

**Mathematical note:** BPE training is a greedy algorithm. At each step it selects the pair (a, b) with maximum frequency and replaces all occurrences. The merge order defines a priority: during encoding, merges are applied in the order they were learned. This is O(merges x N) per encoding call -- acceptable for education, but production tokenizers like tiktoken use a trie-based approach that is O(N).

## Cell 11 -- Section 3: Dataset -- Sliding Windows (Markdown)

> Now that we can convert text to numbers, we need to create training examples. The training objective for a causal language model is beautifully simple: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next one at every position.
> 
> We do this by sliding a window of length `max_seq_len` (256) across the tokenized corpus. Each window gives us an input sequence and a target sequence, where the target is the input shifted right by one position.
> 
> Look at the diagram. If our tokens are A, B, C, D, E, then input = [A, B, C, D] and target = [B, C, D, E]. At position 0, the model sees only A and should predict B. At position 1, it sees A and B and should predict C. At position 2, it sees A, B, C and should predict D. This is the autoregressive training setup.
> 
> The windows overlap by 50 percent -- our stride is `seq_len // 2 = 128`. This means every token-to-token transition in the corpus falls inside some training window, which helps the model learn patterns at the boundaries. Non-overlapping windows would miss those boundary transitions.

**Mathematical formulation:** The training loss is the average cross-entropy over all positions:

L = -(1/T) * sum_{t=1}^{T} log P(x_t | x_{<t})

where P(x_t | x_{<t}) is the model's predicted probability for the correct next token, conditioned on all preceding tokens. This is maximizing the likelihood of the training data under the autoregressive factorization.

## Cell 15 -- RMSNorm Demo (Code)

> *Run the cell.*
> 
> RMSNorm is our first building block. Normalization is critical in deep networks -- without it, activation magnitudes can explode or collapse across layers, making gradient-based optimization unstable.
> 
> The formula is:
> 
> RMSNorm(x) = x / sqrt(mean(x^2) + epsilon) * gamma
> 
> where gamma is a learnable per-dimension scale parameter. Compare this to LayerNorm, which first subtracts the mean (re-centering) and then divides by the standard deviation, with both a scale and a bias parameter. RMSNorm drops the mean subtraction and the bias -- simpler, faster, and empirically equivalent.
> 
> Look at the output. Input has mean near zero and std near 1 (random normal data). After RMSNorm, the RMS of each vector is normalized to approximately 1, scaled by gamma (initialized to 1). RMSNorm has 384 parameters (one scale per dimension); LayerNorm would have 768 (scale plus bias).
> 
> Every production LLM since LLaMA-1 in early 2023 uses RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm. The paper is Zhang and Sennrich (2019), "Root Mean Square Layer Normalization."

**Mathematical detail:** For a vector x of dimension d:

RMS(x) = sqrt((1/d) * sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2)

The normalization divides by RMS(x), which is equivalent to multiplying by rsqrt(mean(x^2) + eps). The epsilon (1e-6) prevents division by zero.

## Cell 16 -- RoPE: Rotary Position Embeddings Header (Markdown)

> Position encoding is one of the most elegant design problems in Transformer architecture. The self-attention mechanism is inherently permutation-equivariant -- if you shuffle the input tokens, the attention weights change but the mechanism has no way to "know" the original order. "The cat sat" and "sat cat the" would produce the same attention patterns. We must inject position information explicitly.
> 
> The original Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) used fixed sinusoidal patterns added to the token embeddings. GPT-2 used learned position embeddings. But modern models use Rotary Position Embeddings -- RoPE -- introduced by Su et al. (2021).
> 
> RoPE works by rotating pairs of dimensions in the Q and K vectors. For each pair of adjacent dimensions (2i, 2i+1), apply a 2D rotation by an angle that depends on the token's position. The rotation angle is pos times theta_i, where theta_i = 10000^(-2i/d_head).
> 
> The key mathematical insight is that the dot product of two rotated vectors Q_m and K_n depends only on the difference (m - n), not on the absolute positions m and n individually. So we encode absolute position but the attention score naturally captures relative position. This is why RoPE generalizes better to sequence lengths longer than those seen during training.

**Mathematical formulation:** For position m and dimension pair i:

theta_i = 10000^(-2i / d_head)

The rotation matrix R(m, i) applied to (x_{2i}, x_{2i+1}) is:

[cos(m * theta_i)  -sin(m * theta_i)] [x_{2i}  ]
[sin(m * theta_i)   cos(m * theta_i)] [x_{2i+1}]

The dot product Q_m^T K_n for this pair equals:

(q_{2i} cos(m*theta) - q_{2i+1} sin(m*theta)) * (k_{2i} cos(n*theta) - k_{2i+1} sin(n*theta))

+ (q_{2i} sin(m*theta) + q_{2i+1} cos(m*theta)) * (k_{2i} sin(n*theta) + k_{2i+1} cos(n*theta))

which simplifies to a function of (m - n) only.

**Research reference:** Su, J., Lu, Y., Pan, S., Murtadha, A., Wen, B., & Liu, Y. (2021). "RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding." arXiv:2104.09864.

## Cell 18 -- Causal Self-Attention Header (Markdown)

> Self-attention is the core mechanism that makes Transformers work. Every other architecture before this -- RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs -- processed sequences sequentially. Self-attention processes all positions in parallel, with each token directly comparing itself to every other token.
> 
> Let me walk through the mechanism step by step, at the level of the linear algebra.
> 
> Each token embedding x_i (dimension d_model) is projected into three vectors through learned linear transformations: a Query q_i, a Key k_i, and a Value v_i. Think of it as a soft dictionary lookup. The Query asks "what am I looking for?" The Key says "this is what I contain." The Value carries "this is my information."
> 
> We compute the compatibility between every pair of tokens using the dot product Q times K-transpose, scaled by the square root of d_head. This produces a T-by-T matrix of raw attention scores.
> 
> Then comes the causal mask: we set all entries above the diagonal to negative infinity. This ensures that position i can only attend to positions 0 through i -- it cannot see the future. When softmax processes negative infinity, it outputs zero, so the causal mask is absolute.
> 
> Softmax converts each row of scores into a probability distribution. Each row sums to 1. High values mean "pay attention to this token," low values mean "ignore it."
> 
> Finally, we multiply the attention weights by V, producing a weighted combination of value vectors at each position. The result: each token's output is a mixture of information from all the tokens it was allowed to attend to.

**Complexity analysis:** The attention computation Q * K^T produces a (T, T) matrix. This is O(T^2 * d_head) for the matrix multiplication, and O(T^2) for the softmax. For our model with T=256 and d_head=64, this is 256^2 * 64 = ~4M FLOPs per head, or ~24M across 6 heads. At T=128,000 (GPT-4's context), this becomes 128K^2 * 128 = ~2T FLOPs per head -- which is why techniques like FlashAttention and sliding-window attention exist.

**Research reference:** Vaswani, A., Shazeer, N., Parmar, N., Uszkoreit, J., Jones, L., Gomez, A. N., Kaiser, L., & Polosukhin, I. (2017). "Attention Is All You Need." NeurIPS.

**Common pitfall:** A frequent source of bugs is the causal mask dimensions. The mask must be (1, 1, max_seq_len, max_seq_len) and sliced to (1, 1, T, T) during forward. The test `test_causal_mask_no_future_leakage` in the test suite verifies this by perturbing token T-1 and checking that logits at positions 0 through T-2 remain unchanged.

## Cell 20 -- SwiGLU Feed-Forward Network Header (Markdown)

> After attention routes information between positions, the feed-forward network computes on it position-wise. If attention is the routing mechanism -- deciding what information flows where -- the FFN is the computation engine.
> 
> Research by Geva et al. (2021) showed that FFN layers act as key-value memories. The first weight matrix stores patterns (keys), and the second stores corresponding knowledge (values). When the model "knows" that Paris is the capital of France, that knowledge is encoded in FFN weight matrices.
> 
> We use SwiGLU, proposed by Shazeer (2020). Instead of the standard ReLU(x * W1) * W2 pattern, SwiGLU uses:
> 
> output = SiLU(x * W_gate) * (x * W_up) * W_down
> 
> where SiLU is the Sigmoid Linear Unit: SiLU(x) = x * sigmoid(x), also called "swish." The gating mechanism -- multiplying the SiLU-activated gate path by the linear up path -- gives the model a learned, element-wise filter. The gate decides what information to keep; the up projection provides the information to be filtered.
> 
> Because SwiGLU has three weight matrices instead of two, we reduce the hidden dimension to 2/3 of d_ff (and round to a multiple of 8 for GPU kernel efficiency) to keep the total parameter count comparable to a standard two-matrix FFN.

**Research reference:** Shazeer, N. (2020). "GLU Variants Improve Transformer." arXiv:2002.05202. The paper shows SwiGLU consistently outperforms ReLU and GELU activations across multiple benchmarks.

> Three weight matrices: gate_proj (384 x 1024), up_proj (384 x 1024), and down_proj (1024 x 384). The gate and up projections expand the representation to 1024 dimensions, where element-wise gating happens, and down_proj compresses back.
> 
> The total FFN parameter count for one block is about 3 * 384 * 1024 = roughly 1.18 million parameters. Across 6 layers, the FFN accounts for about 7 million of our 15 million total parameters -- nearly half the model. This is typical: in most Transformers, the FFN contains more parameters than the attention mechanism. In LLaMA-2 7B, the FFN accounts for roughly two-thirds of all parameters.

## Cell 22 -- TransformerBlock and Pre-Norm Header (Markdown)

> Now we assemble attention and FFN into a complete Transformer block. The key design choice is Pre-Norm -- we normalize BEFORE each sublayer, not after. Combined with the residual connection, the pattern is:
> 
> x = x + Attention(RMSNorm(x))
> x = x + FFN(RMSNorm(x))
> 
> This ordering was first used in GPT-2 and is now universal in all modern LLMs. The original Transformer (Vaswani 2017) used Post-Norm: LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)). Post-Norm was harder to train for deep networks because gradients had to flow through the normalization during backpropagation, which could amplify or suppress them unpredictably. Pre-Norm lets gradients flow directly through the residual path -- the identity shortcut -- providing a clean gradient highway from the output all the way back to the input.

## Cell 24 -- Forward Pass Sanity Check (Code)

> *Run the cell.*
> 
> We feed random token IDs through the model and check the loss. The loss should be close to ln(vocab_size) -- for vocab_size 4096, that is approximately 8.3.
> 
> Why? A randomly initialized model assigns roughly uniform probability to all tokens. The cross-entropy loss of a uniform distribution over N items is -log(1/N) = log(N). If your initial loss is significantly different from log(vocab_size), something is wrong with the architecture -- a common symptom is an incorrect vocab_size, a missing normalization, or a broken logit computation.
> 
> This is a universal sanity check. Every time you build a new model architecture, before training, verify that the initial loss matches the theoretical random baseline. It takes one forward pass and catches a wide category of bugs.

**Mathematical detail:** Cross-entropy loss: L = -(1/N) * sum log(p_correct). With uniform probabilities p_correct = 1/V for all positions: L = -log(1/V) = log(V). For V = 4096: L = log(4096) = 8.318.

## Cell 29 -- Attention Mechanism Step-by-Step (Code)

> *Run the cell.*
> 
> This is the most important visualization in the entire notebook. Three heatmaps showing the attention mechanism from raw scores to final weights.
> 
> **Left panel: Raw scores.** This is Q times K-transpose, divided by sqrt(64). It is a full T-by-T matrix -- every token has a score for every other token, including future tokens. The values are real numbers, not yet probabilities. Large positive values mean "these tokens are highly compatible." Negative values mean "these tokens are irrelevant to each other."
> 
> The scaling by sqrt(d_head) is crucial. Without it, the dot products grow proportionally to d_head. For d_head = 64, the dot products would have variance around 64, pushing softmax into near-zero gradient saturation. Dividing by sqrt(64) = 8 keeps the variance around 1, keeping softmax in its useful operating range. This was explained in the original Transformer paper.
> 
> **Middle panel: After causal mask.** The upper triangle is now grey -- those positions have been set to negative infinity. The model cannot attend to future tokens. This is the fundamental constraint that makes autoregressive generation possible.
> 
> **Right panel: After softmax.** Now each row is a valid probability distribution that sums to 1. Bright cells indicate strong attention. This is literally what happens inside the model every time it processes a token. At every layer, at every head, a matrix like this is computed.
> 
> Look for patterns. Does the last token ("the") attend strongly to any particular earlier token? Are there vertical stripes indicating tokens that attract attention from everyone? These patterns become more meaningful after training.
> 
> *Pause for questions. This is the key conceptual moment of the lecture.*

**Scaling rationale:** If each element of q and k is drawn from N(0, 1), then q^T k has variance d_head (sum of d_head products of independent normals). For d_head = 64, this means scores have std ~8, which pushes softmax into saturation (one entry near 1, all others near 0), causing vanishing gradients. Dividing by sqrt(d_head) normalizes the variance to 1.

## Cell 31 -- Section 6: Training (Markdown)

> Time to train the model. We have the architecture, we have the data pipeline, now we need to optimize the 15 million parameters so the model actually learns to predict the next token in TinyStories text.
> 
> The training loop has several important components, each addressing a specific practical challenge:
> 
> **AdamW optimizer.** Adam with decoupled weight decay. We use separate parameter groups: 2D weight matrices (attention projections, FFN matrices) get weight decay of 0.1, while 1D parameters (norms, biases) get zero weight decay. Weight decay is a form of regularization that pulls weights toward zero, but applying it to normalization parameters would counteract their purpose.
> 
> **Linear warmup plus cosine decay.** The learning rate starts at zero, ramps up linearly over 200 steps, then follows a cosine curve down to 10 percent of the peak. Warmup prevents early instability when gradients are noisy and large. Cosine decay is smoother than step decay and avoids sudden jumps.
> 
> **Mixed precision.** We use bf16 (or fp16 on older GPUs) for the forward and backward passes via PyTorch's `autocast` and `GradScaler`. This halves memory usage and roughly doubles throughput with negligible impact on convergence.
> 
> **Gradient clipping.** We clip the global gradient norm to 1.0. This is cheap insurance against occasional gradient explosions that can destabilize training, especially in the early phase.

## Cell 32 -- Learning Rate Schedule Visualization (Code)

> *Run the cell.*
> 
> This is the learning rate schedule over 1000 hypothetical steps. The red dashed line marks where warmup ends at step 200. Before that, the LR increases linearly from zero to the peak of 3e-4. After warmup, it follows a cosine curve that asymptotically approaches 10 percent of the peak: 3e-5.
> 
> Why warmup? At initialization, the model's weights are random. The gradients computed from random weights are noisy and can have large magnitude. If we immediately apply a high learning rate to these large, noisy gradients, the resulting parameter updates are huge and erratic, often causing the loss to spike or diverge. By starting with a near-zero learning rate and gradually increasing it, we give the model time to find a sensible region of parameter space before taking large steps.
> 
> Why cosine decay? As training progresses and the model approaches a local minimum, we want smaller updates. A step schedule (sudden LR drops) can cause training instability at the transition. Cosine decay provides a smooth, gradual reduction that works well empirically.

## Cell 35 -- Section 7: Text Generation (Markdown)

> Now the payoff -- using our trained model to generate text. The model predicts one token at a time. We take the predicted token, append it to the sequence, and feed the extended sequence back through the model to predict the next token. This autoregressive loop continues until we have enough text.
> 
> We have two generation methods. `generate()` is the straightforward approach: at each step, it processes the entire sequence from scratch. `generate_fast()` uses a KV cache -- it stores the Key and Value tensors from previous tokens and only computes the new token's contribution. Both produce the same output (up to floating-point rounding), but the cached version avoids redundant computation.
> 
> The sampling parameters control how we select the next token from the model's predicted probability distribution:
> 
> **Temperature** scales the logits before softmax. Temperature less than 1 makes the distribution sharper (more deterministic), temperature greater than 1 makes it flatter (more random). At temperature approaching 0, sampling converges to greedy (always pick the most likely token).
> 
> **Top-k** restricts the candidate set to the k most likely tokens. Everything else gets zero probability. This prevents the model from ever choosing extremely unlikely tokens.
> 
> **Top-p (nucleus sampling)** dynamically determines the candidate set. It includes the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds p. For a confident prediction (one token has 95 percent probability), only that token is considered. For an uncertain prediction (many tokens with similar probabilities), many tokens are candidates. This was introduced by Holtzman et al. (2020).

## Cell 45 -- Section 9: KV Cache Deep Dive (Markdown)

> The KV cache is arguably the single most important optimization in production LLM deployment. Without it, generating text at interactive speeds would be economically infeasible.
> 
> Here is the problem. Standard autoregressive generation works like this: to generate token N, you feed all N tokens through the model to get the prediction for token N+1. To generate token N+1, you feed all N+1 tokens through -- recomputing attention over all previous tokens. The total work to generate M new tokens from a prompt of length P is:
> 
> sum_{t=P}^{P+M} O(t^2 * d) = O((P+M)^3 * d / 3)
> 
> That is cubic in the total sequence length. For a 4000-token context, that is 64 billion operations just for the attention score computation.
> 
> With a KV cache, we store the Key and Value vectors from all previous tokens. When generating the new token, we compute Q, K, V only for that single new token. We concatenate the new K and V with the cached ones and compute attention: one query row against all cached keys. Each step is O(T * d) instead of O(T^2 * d). Total work becomes O(M * (P+M) * d) -- quadratic in the total but linear per step.
> 
> The implementation requires four changes to the model: (1) RoPE must accept a `start_pos` parameter so the new token gets its correct absolute position angle. (2) CausalSelfAttention must accept and return past_kv cache tensors. (3) TransformerBlock threads the cache through. (4) NanoLLM collects per-layer caches.
> 
> The critical invariant: the RoPE angle for token at absolute position t must always be the same, whether we compute it during a full forward pass or during a single-token decode step with cache. This means `start_pos = past_sequence_length`. Getting this wrong is the number one bug in KV cache implementations -- the model generates fluently but nonsensically because every decode token thinks it is at position 0.

**Complexity analysis:**

| Method   | Per-step cost | Total for M tokens           |
| -------- | ------------- | ---------------------------- |
| No cache | O(T^2 * d)    | O(M * T^2 * d) where T grows |
| KV cache | O(T * d)      | O(M * T * d) where T grows   |

For our model: d_head=64, 6 heads, 6 layers. Generating 100 tokens from a 200-token prompt, the cache saves roughly 99% of the attention computation.

## Appendix A: Mathematical Reference

### A.1 RMSNorm

$$\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2 + \epsilon}} \cdot \gamma$$

where gamma is a learnable scale vector of dimension d, and epsilon = 1e-6 prevents division by zero.

### A.2 Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)

For dimension pair (2i, 2i+1) at position m:

$$\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d_{\text{head}}}$$

$$\begin{pmatrix} q'_{2i} \\ q'_{2i+1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(m\theta_i) & -\sin(m\theta_i) \\ \sin(m\theta_i) & \cos(m\theta_i) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} q_{2i} \\ q_{2i+1} \end{pmatrix}$$

Key property: q_m^T * k_n depends only on (m - n), not on m and n individually.

### A.3 Scaled Dot-Product Attention

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

With causal mask M (lower triangular):

$$\text{score}_{ij} = \begin{cases} q_i^T k_j / \sqrt{d_k} & \text{if } j \leq i \\ -\infty & \text{if } j > i \end{cases}$$

### A.4 Multi-Head Attention

$$\text{head}_h = \text{Attention}(XW_Q^h, XW_K^h, XW_V^h)$$

$$\text{MultiHead}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_H) \cdot W_O$$

### A.5 SwiGLU Feed-Forward

$$\text{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(x) = (\text{SiLU}(xW_{\text{gate}}) \odot xW_{\text{up}}) \cdot W_{\text{down}}$$

where SiLU(x) = x * sigmoid(x) and the symbol represents element-wise multiplication.

### A.6 Pre-Norm Transformer Block

$$x = x + \text{Attention}(\text{RMSNorm}(x))$$

$$x = x + \text{FFN}(\text{RMSNorm}(x))$$

### A.7 Cross-Entropy Loss (Causal LM)

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

where P_theta is the model's predicted probability for the correct token.

### A.8 Weight Tying

The embedding matrix E (V x d_model) and the output projection W_lm (d_model x V) share the same tensor:

$$W_{\text{lm}} = E^T$$

This reduces parameters by V * d_model and enforces consistency between input and output token representations.

### A.9 Cosine Learning Rate Schedule with Warmup

$$\text{lr}(t) = \begin{cases} \text{lr}_{\text{peak}} \cdot \frac{t}{t_{\text{warmup}}} & \text{if } t < t_{\text{warmup}} \\ \text{lr}_{\text{min}} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{lr}_{\text{peak}} - \text{lr}_{\text{min}})(1 + \cos(\frac{t - t_{\text{warmup}}}{t_{\text{total}} - t_{\text{warmup}}} \pi)) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where lr_min = 0.1 * lr_peak.

## Appendix B: Scaling Comparison

| Dimension         | NanoLLM (ours) | LLaMA-2 7B   | LLaMA-3 70B   | GPT-4 (est.)    |
| ----------------- | -------------- | ------------ | ------------- | --------------- |
| Parameters        | ~15M           | 7B           | 70B           | ~1.8T (MoE)     |
| n_layers          | 6              | 32           | 80            | 120+            |
| d_model           | 384            | 4096         | 8192          | 12288+          |
| n_heads           | 6              | 32           | 64            | 96+             |
| d_head            | 64             | 128          | 128           | 128             |
| d_ff              | 1536           | 11008        | 28672         | ~49152          |
| Context length    | 256            | 4096         | 8192          | 128K            |
| Vocab size        | ~4096          | 32000        | 128256        | ~100K           |
| Training tokens   | ~350K          | 2T           | 15T           | 13T+            |
| Training compute  | 1 GPU-min      | 184K GPU-hrs | ~1.7M GPU-hrs | ~25K A100-years |
| Norm              | RMSNorm        | RMSNorm      | RMSNorm       | RMSNorm         |
| Position encoding | RoPE           | RoPE         | RoPE          | RoPE            |
| FFN activation    | SwiGLU         | SwiGLU       | SwiGLU        | SwiGLU (est.)   |
| Attention         | MHA            | GQA          | GQA           | MHA or GQA      |

**What changes at scale:**

- **Grouped Query Attention (GQA):** Share K/V heads across groups of Q heads. LLaMA-2 7B uses 32 Q heads but only 32 KV heads (standard MHA); LLaMA-2 70B uses 64 Q heads but only 8 KV groups, reducing KV cache memory by 8x.
- **Mixture of Experts (MoE):** Only activate a subset of FFN parameters per token. GPT-4 reportedly uses MoE, activating ~280B of 1.8T parameters per forward pass.
- **RLHF / RLAIF:** Align model behavior with human preferences via reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback. All production chatbots use this.
- **Context extension:** YaRN, Dynamic NTK, ALiBi for sequences longer than training length.
- **Infrastructure:** FSDP, tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, ZeRO optimizer for distributing across thousands of GPUs.

**What stays exactly the same:**

- The Transformer block structure (Pre-Norm + attention + FFN + residual)
- The self-attention mechanism (scaled dot-product with causal mask)
- The autoregressive training objective (next-token prediction with cross-entropy)
- BPE-family tokenization
- KV caching for inference
- AdamW optimizer family

## Appendix C: Further Reading

### Foundational Papers

1. **Vaswani, A. et al. (2017).** "Attention Is All You Need." NeurIPS.
   -- The original Transformer paper. Introduced scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, and the encoder-decoder Transformer architecture.

2. **Su, J. et al. (2021).** "RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding." arXiv:2104.09864.
   -- Introduced RoPE. The key insight: encoding position via rotation gives relative position awareness through the dot product, with better length generalization than learned or sinusoidal encodings.

3. **Zhang, B. & Sennrich, R. (2019).** "Root Mean Square Layer Normalization." NeurIPS.
   -- Showed RMSNorm achieves comparable performance to LayerNorm with fewer operations and parameters. Now standard in all LLaMA-family models.

4. **Shazeer, N. (2020).** "GLU Variants Improve Transformer." arXiv:2002.05202.
   -- Systematic comparison of gated linear unit variants. SwiGLU (Swish-gated) consistently outperforms ReLU, GELU, and other activations. Adopted by LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen.

5. **Press, O. & Wolf, L. (2017).** "Using the Output Embedding to Improve Language Models." EACL.
   -- Weight tying between input embeddings and output projection. Reduces parameters and improves quality by enforcing consistent input/output representations.

### Dataset and Evaluation

6. **Eldan, R. & Li, Y. (2023).** "TinyStories: How Small Can Language Models Be and Still Speak Coherent English?" arXiv:2305.07759.
   -- Introduced the TinyStories dataset. Showed that models under 30M parameters can generate fluent, grammatically correct stories when trained on appropriately simple data. Our training corpus.

### Textbook

7. **Raschka, S. (2024).** "Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)." Manning.
   -- The textbook this notebook is aligned with. Chapters 2-5 cover the material we present here (tokenization, embeddings, attention, training, generation), using the slightly older GPT-2 conventions (LayerNorm, learned position embeddings, GELU).

### Architecture and Interpretability

8. **Touvron, H. et al. (2023).** "LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models." arXiv:2302.13971.
   -- Introduced the modern architecture recipe (RMSNorm + RoPE + SwiGLU + Pre-Norm) that NanoLLM follows, scaled to 7B-65B parameters.

9. **Elhage, N. et al. (2021).** "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits." Anthropic.
   -- Foundational work on mechanistic interpretability. Analyzes attention heads as circuits and identifies specific head types (induction heads, previous-token heads).

10. **Geva, M. et al. (2021).** "Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Are Key-Value Memories." EMNLP.
    -- Shows that FFN layers store factual knowledge as key-value pairs, explaining why FFN parameters dominate model size.

### Inference Optimization

11. **Pope, R. et al. (2022).** "Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference." MLSys.
    -- Analyzes KV cache memory, compute, and bandwidth bottlenecks in production LLM serving. Explains why KV cache management is the central challenge in LLM deployment.

### Tokenization

12. **Sennrich, R., Haddow, B., & Birch, A. (2016).** "Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units." ACL.
    -- Introduced BPE for NLP. The algorithm was originally a data compression technique (Gage, 1994) adapted for subword segmentation.

### Sampling

13. **Holtzman, A. et al. (2020).** "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration." ICLR.
    -- Introduced nucleus (top-p) sampling. Showed that likelihood-maximizing decoding (beam search, greedy) produces degenerate, repetitive text, while top-p sampling better matches human text statistics.
